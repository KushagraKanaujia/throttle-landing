FP8 looked 47% cheaper. It wasn't.

vLLM's on-the-fly FP8 made a 72B model look 47% cheaper per token and a 32B look 41% cheaper. Both times the answers had changed shape; the 32B answered "!!!!". The FP8 checkpoint that really did cut 32%, and the check I run before trusting any FP8 saving.

Kushagra KanaujiaFounder, ThrottleOct 23, 20265 min read

I turned on one vLLM flag and my 72B model's cost per token dropped from $1.67 to $0.88 per million output tokens. That's 47% cheaper, from a single line: --quantization fp8.

I didn't keep it. The answers had changed.

This is what happened on rented AMD MI300X GPUs, how a much bigger "saving" turned out to be the same flag breaking the model, and the FP8 setup that did give a real cut.

The setup

One AMD MI300X rented from Hot Aisle at its $2.99/GPU-hour list price (an ASSUMED rate, not an invoice). vLLM on ROCm, version 0.23.1. Qwen2.5-Instruct at 7B, 32B and 72B. Throttle's default workload: 32 concurrent requests, max 256 output tokens, temperature 0, cold prompt cache. Each config was checked three or four times so the run-to-run noise was measured before any comparison.

At temperature 0 the same prompts should get the same answers. That's what makes this test work: if a config change makes answers much longer or shorter, something other than speed changed.

What the dashboard saw

Model, change Before After Looks like
72B, on-the-fly FP8 $1.67 $0.88 −47%
32B, on-the-fly FP8 $0.76 $0.45 −41%

Both results were well outside the noise: three of three checks each. A cost tool that only looks at dollars and noise would call both of them verified savings.

What actually happened

Look at how many tokens each block produced. A block is 32 requests with a 256-token cap, so the most it can ever produce is 32 × 256 = 8,192 tokens.

Model BF16 tokens per block On-the-fly FP8 tokens per block
72B 6,316 to 6,389 8,013 to 8,192
32B 6,699 to 7,072 8,192, every block

With FP8 on, nearly every request ran straight to the cap. Output length per request went up about 28 to 29% on the 72B. On the 32B every single request hit the cap.

Then I read the answers. On the 32B, I asked it to summarize the causes of World War I in about 80 words. BF16 answered in 89 tokens and stopped. The FP8 server answered with this, until it hit max_tokens:

!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!

That's where the "saving" came from. A server producing exclamation marks at full batch is very efficient per token. The tokens were cheap because they were worthless.

For the 72B I didn't sample the on-the-fly FP8 answers, so I won't tell you what they said. What I can tell you is that they changed shape: much longer, nearly all at the cap, on prompts that should have produced the same output as before. That's enough to refuse it as a saving.

The FP8 that worked

FP8 itself isn't the problem. Same GPU, same day, a pre-quantized FP8 checkpoint instead of quantizing on the fly:

Model, change Before After Verdict
72B, pre-quantized FP8 (RedHatAI/Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct-FP8-dynamic) $1.67 $1.13 CHEAPER, −32%
7B, pre-quantized FP8 (RedHatAI/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-FP8-dynamic) $0.23 $0.19 CHEAPER, about −18%

On the 72B, output length per request stayed within 1% of BF16, all three checks agreed (−32.4% to −32.5% against a noise bound under 1%), and 6 of 6 sample answers were correct and coherent. On the 7B, the sample answers were correct too. One caveat on the 72B: the FP8 server ran on the VM's second MI300X and the BF16 baseline on the first. Same GPU model, but not the same card.

So the honest version of the FP8 story is: it can cut a third off your cost per token, or it can hand you a 47% "saving" that is really a broken model. Same flag family, opposite outcomes. On the 7B, even on-the-fly FP8 was fine (output length up 6 to 9%, answers correct). You can't know which case you're in without checking.

What Throttle does about it

After this run I added a verdict: OUTPUT CHANGED. When the same prompts at temperature 0 come back more than 10% longer or shorter, or many more of them hit max_tokens, Throttle won't call the change CHEAPER, however good the dollars look. Both on-the-fly FP8 results above get OUTPUT CHANGED, 3 of 3.

Be clear about what that check is. It looks at answer length and token-cap hits. It does not grade answers. It caught the "!!!!" case because broken output ran to the cap. A quantized model that gives wrong answers of the right length would pass it. Keep your eval suite; this is the guard that stops a cost dashboard from rewarding a broken model, not a replacement for evals.

What I do before turning on FP8

  1. Prefer a published pre-quantized FP8 checkpoint over --quantization fp8 on the fly, and measure both if you can.
  2. Price BF16 and FP8 in dollars per million tokens on your own traffic, with repeat checks for noise.
  3. Compare output lengths and cap hits at temperature 0. A big jump means stop and read.
  4. Read a handful of answers from both. Then run your real evals.
  5. If FP8 suddenly looks much cheaper than you expected, be suspicious before you celebrate.

The longer reference, with the cost-versus-quality trade-off by model size, is in the guide FP8 quantization in vLLM: cost vs quality.

Caveats

One GPU type, one engine build, one model family, one workload, one session. The $2.99/hr is an assumed list price. Raw check records and answer samples are public under validation/hotaisle-mi300x-20261001, and every row is on the Cost Index. These are results for this setup, not a savings projection for yours.

Cutting what teams pay for inference on their own GPUs is the whole business: we only get paid from savings that hold up. A saving that comes from a broken model is not one, which is why it never counts.
