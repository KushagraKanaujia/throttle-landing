How to price a token on your own GPUs (the formula)

The formula for dollars per million tokens on GPUs you own or rent, worked through with a real MI300X measurement, and the four inputs that make it wrong: GPU count, the hourly rate for owned GPUs, idle time, and which tokens you divide by.

Kushagra KanaujiaFounder, ThrottleOct 30, 20266 min read

If you run models on your own GPUs, nobody sends you a per-token price. You get a GPU bill, or a hardware budget, and a pile of tokens per second numbers. To decide anything about cost, you have to turn one into the other yourself.

Here's the formula I use, worked through with a real measurement from rented AMD MI300X GPUs, plus the four inputs people most often get wrong: GPU count, the hourly rate for GPUs you own, idle time, and which tokens you divide by.

The formula

$/M output tokens = (GPU $/hr × GPUs × wall-clock hours) ÷ output tokens × 1,000,000

Everything on the top line is what you paid for the time. Everything on the bottom is what you got for it. Two of the inputs are measured (wall-clock time and tokens) and two are decided by you (the hourly rate and the GPU count). Be clear in your own notes about which is which.

A real example: one GPU

This is one measured block from my Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct run on vLLM, one MI300X, 32 concurrent requests, max 256 output tokens, priced at Hot Aisle's $2.99/GPU-hour list price:

  • Output tokens in the block: 6,321
  • Wall-clock time for the block: 12.777 s = 0.0035493 hours
GPU dollars  = $2.99 × 1 × 0.0035493 h        = $0.010612
$/M output   = $0.010612 ÷ 6,321 × 1,000,000  = $1.679

That block cost $1.68 per million output tokens. Across all four repeat checks of this config, the average was $1.67.

The same thing written as throughput, which is often easier to sanity-check: the block ran at 494.7 output tokens per second, or about 1.78 million tokens per GPU-hour. $2.99 for 1.78 million tokens is $1.68 per million. If your two calculations disagree, one of your inputs is wrong.

Input 1: GPU count

Here's a block from the same model on two GPUs with --tensor-parallel-size 2:

  • Output tokens: 6,311
  • Wall-clock time: 8.192 s
GPU dollars  = $2.99 × 2 × (8.192 ÷ 3600) h   = $0.013608
$/M output   = $0.013608 ÷ 6,311 × 1,000,000  = $2.156

Faster block, more expensive tokens. Now see what happens if you forget the second GPU and price it at $2.99:

$2.99 × 1 × (8.192 ÷ 3600) h ÷ 6,311 × 1,000,000 = $1.078

That wrong number says two GPUs are 36% cheaper than one. The right one says they're about 28% more expensive (averaged over all the checks: $1.67 vs $2.14). This is not a hypothetical mistake: an early version of my own tool made it. Multiply by every GPU that serves the endpoint, including the ones that sit in a tensor-parallel group.

Input 2: the hourly rate for GPUs you own

Rented GPUs are easy: use the hourly price you actually pay, per GPU. The MI300X example uses the list price, and I label it ASSUMED because it's not an invoice.

For GPUs you own, there's no meter, so build an hourly rate and write down how:

owned GPU $/hr = hardware cost ÷ useful-life hours
               + power and cooling per hour
               + hosting, network and ops per hour

Pick a useful life you actually believe, and be consistent. The point isn't a perfect number. It's using the same number for every config you compare, so the comparison is fair. A cheaper config on an assumed rate is still cheaper on the real one, as long as the GPU count is counted.

Input 3: idle time

A measured block assumes the GPU is busy the whole time. Your bill doesn't. If you pay for GPUs around the clock and traffic only keeps them busy part of the day, the idle hours are paid for too, and they land on the tokens you did serve:

real $/M = measured $/M ÷ share of paid hours the GPU is busy

A GPU that's busy half the hours you pay for makes every token cost twice the measured figure. That's why two kinds of savings exist: a config that's cheaper per busy hour (what you measure with a benchmark), and serving the same traffic on fewer GPU-hours (what shows up on the bill). The second needs the first, but it isn't the same thing.

Input 4: which tokens you divide by

The same GPU dollars can be divided by output tokens, input tokens, or both. In that one-GPU block:

Divide the $0.010612 by Tokens $/M
Output tokens 6,321 $1.68
Input tokens 1,436 $7.39
Input + output 7,757 $1.37

None of these is "the" price. Each one puts all of the GPU time on one kind of token. Output tokens are the usual choice for short prompts with long answers, because generation is where the time goes. If your workload is long prompts and short answers (RAG, classification, agents re-reading context), output-only will look scary and total may be the fairer figure. Pick one, say which one, and never compare across them.

The workload is part of the price

The same server has a different price at a different load. Concurrency, prompt length, answer length and whether the prompt cache is warm all change how many tokens you get per GPU-second. My example is 32 concurrent requests, short prompts, answers capped at 256 tokens, cold cache. Measure at the concurrency and prompt shape you actually serve, or your number prices a workload you don't have.

Measure it more than once

The formula gives you one number from one run. Run it again without changing anything and you'll get a different one. On the MI300X the four repeats landed within half a percent of each other. On a laptop I once got a 27% swing with nothing changed. Before you believe that a config change saved money, run the unchanged config at least three times and make sure the change is bigger than that spread.
